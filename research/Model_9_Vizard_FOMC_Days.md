# MODEL 9 — "VIZARD"
### FOMC Day Price Delivery — A Two-Faced Event, Branded for What It Actually Is
**43 FOMC decisions tested — all 8 meetings in 2018, all 8 in 2022, and all meetings 2023 through July 2026, verified against the Federal Reserve's official release schedule. This is a larger sample than either NFP (38) or CPI (42), achieved by pulling separate NQ data for 2018 and 2022 specifically for this test, since the core 2023–2026 dataset alone would only cover ~27 meetings. DXY comparisons are limited to the 27 meetings from 2023–2026, since DXY data isn't available for the 2018/2022 extension.**

---

## Why "Vizard"

A Vizard carries two natures at once — Shinigami and Hollow — that don't act as one unified will. They coexist, and which one is in control at a given moment isn't fixed or predictable from the other. That's the precise, tested shape of FOMC's own structure: a statement at 2:00, a press conference at 2:30, and — per the Two-Stage Structure finding below — no reliable relationship between what one face does and what the other does. This isn't a metaphor stretched to fit; it's the mechanism the data already showed, now named for what it is.

---

## Why FOMC Needed Its Own Model, Not a Blend of the Other Two

FOMC shares surface features with both NFP and CPI — a scheduled macro release capable of moving markets sharply. But it differs from both in one structural way that changes everything downstream: **the decision lands at 2:00 PM ET, not 8:30 AM, and it comes in two distinct parts** — a written statement at 2:00, followed by a live press conference at 2:30. Nearly every finding below traces back to that timing difference.

---

## Session Character — The Most Extreme "Quiet Before, Explosive During" Shape of the Three Events

| Session | FOMC vs. baseline | *(NFP)* | *(CPI)* |
|---|---|---|---|
| Asia | **151.2% — wider** | *93.8%* | *124.5%* |
| London | 74.6% — quiet | *86.2%* | *88.8%* |
| **NY AM (pre-release)** | **67.2% — substantially quieter** | *120.0%* | *106.5%* |
| Pre-release PM (1:30–2:00) | 85.9% — quiet | — | — |
| **Statement window (2:00–2:30)** | **254.8%** | — | — |
| **Press conference window (2:30–4:14)** | **282.8%** | — | — |

**This is the cleanest, most dramatic version of the "coiled positioning, then release" shape found across all three events.** NY AM — the entire morning leading into the decision — runs at only two-thirds of its normal range, consistent with the well-known trading culture of sitting on hands ahead of the Fed. Then the two release windows run at roughly 2.5x and nearly 3x their own baseline. Neither NFP nor CPI comes close to this degree of pre-release suppression or post-release concentration.

---

## A Tight Asia Session Is a Leading Signal for the Whole Day — Except the Statement Itself

The session-character table above shows Asia running wider than baseline *on average* — but averages hide the fact that FOMC's own Asia sessions split into two real groups, and which group a given FOMC day falls into carries information about everything that follows it. Splitting all 40 FOMC days with valid Asia data at the general 2023–2026 Asia baseline (101.2pts) produces 29 "tight/equal" days and 11 "wide" days.

**Every subsequent session scales down when Asia is tight — not just Asia itself:**

| Session | Tight-Asia avg range | Wide-Asia avg range | Ratio (tight ÷ wide) |
|---|---|---|---|
| London | 80pts | 149pts | **53.8%** |
| NY AM | 107pts | 197pts | **54.4%** |
| Pre-release PM | 37pts | 61pts | 60.6% |
| **Statement (2:00–2:30)** | **127pts** | 142pts | **89.2% — barely compressed** |
| Press Conf (2:30–4:14) | 256pts | 332pts | 77.2% |

**This is a genuinely quieter day end to end, not a locally quiet overnight session that gets made up for later.** London and NY AM compress the most — roughly halved relative to the wide-Asia group — and the net-move figures tell the identical story at every session (London: 33 vs. 76 avg |net|; press conference: 130 vs. 201). The one clear exception is the statement window itself, which barely compresses at all (89.2%). **The raw catalyst doesn't care how quiet the overnight session was — the build-up around it does.** The press conference sits in between, compressed but less severely than the sessions that precede the release.

**A secondary, real but less certain finding: tight-Asia days lean more bearish than Vizard's overall down-lean, while wide-Asia days lean bullish.**

| | RTH up-close rate |
|---|---|
| Tight/equal Asia (n=29) | 37.9% |
| Wide Asia (n=11) | 63.6% |

Worth noting, but held more loosely than the session-scaling finding above — the wide-Asia group is only 11 days, and a split this size deserves a wider confidence interval than the point estimate alone suggests.

## Sample Composition — What Includes 2018/2022, and a Direct Robustness Check

Not every figure in this document draws on the same years, and one of them turned out to be meaningfully skewed by that difference. Worth stating plainly before anything else:

| Uses 2018 + 2022 + 2023–2026 (n=43) | 2023–2026 only (n=27) |
|---|---|
| Session character, directional character, two-stage structure, gap fill, disruption mechanic, next-day NY AM tests | DXY comparisons (no DXY data exists for 2018/2022) |

**Every non-DXY finding in this document was tested for cross-period consistency by recomputing it on 2018 only, 2022 only, and 2023–2026 only, separately.** Two findings held up consistently; one did not.

**Gap fill and the disruption-mechanic test are robust across all three periods:**

| | 2018 only (n≤8) | 2022 only (n=8) | 2023–2026 only (n=27) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full gap fill | 80.0% | 62.5% | 75.0% |
| Week-direction match (disruption test) | 50.0% | 37.5% | 48.1% |

Wide, overlapping intervals at these small subsample sizes, but no period pulls sharply away from the others — these two findings are genuine cross-regime patterns, not artifacts of any single year.

**The directional down-lean is not robust — it's substantially a 2018 artifact:**

| | 2018 only (n=8) | 2022 only (n=8) | 2023–2026 only (n=27) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Up-close frequency | **12.5%** (1 of 8) | 50.0% | 48.1% |
| Avg. net move | −34.8 | −0.5 | −30.9 |
| Avg. absolute displacement | **36.0 — far smaller** | **278.7 — far larger** | 190.2 |

**2018 alone shows an extreme down-lean (1 up-close in 8 meetings) that neither 2022 nor 2023–2026 comes close to reproducing — both of those sit at essentially a coin flip (50.0%, 48.1%).** The combined 41.9% figure reported below is real as a description of the pooled 43-event sample, but it should not be read as "FOMC reliably closes down more often than not" — that specific characterization is being carried disproportionately by eight meetings from a single year. **Treat the down-lean as unconfirmed outside of 2018 specifically**, while the larger-than-baseline displacement finding is real but comes from a different source than expected — 2022's extreme volatility (278.7 avg, nearly double 2023–2026's own 190.2), not a uniform effect across regimes.

---

## Directional Character — The Only One of the Three Events With a Downward Lean, and the Weakest-Supported Finding Here

| | FOMC | Baseline | *(NFP)* | *(CPI)* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Up-close frequency | **41.9%** (CI 28.4–56.7%) | 53.9% | *63.2%* | *61.9%* |
| Average net move | **−26.0** | +3.4 | *−7.3* | *+11.8* |
| Average absolute displacement | **178.0 — larger** | 136.2 | *~21% above baseline* | *smaller than baseline* |

**This is a genuinely distinct signature in the pooled 43-event sample — but per the robustness check directly above, it's the one finding in this document that doesn't hold up when tested period by period.** Both NFP and CPI lean toward more frequent up-closes; FOMC's pooled figure leans the other way, but that lean is carried almost entirely by 2018's eight meetings. 2022 and 2023–2026 each sit close to a coin flip on their own. Hold this one more loosely than everything else in this document.

---

## The Two-Stage Structure Is Real Mechanically, But Shows No Reversal Pattern

FOMC is the only one of the three events with a genuine two-part catalyst — the written statement, then Powell's live remarks 30 minutes later. The natural hypothesis is that the press conference often reverses the market's initial read of the statement.

| | FOMC | *(NFP's analogous two-stage test)* | *(CPI's)* |
|---|---|---|---|
| Second stage opposes first stage | **51.2%** (CI 36.8–65.4%) — a coin flip | *65.8%* | *59.5%* |

**Despite having a real, distinct second catalyst — unlike NFP and CPI's arbitrary time-based split — FOMC shows no reversal tendency at all.** This is worth sitting with: the market's reaction to Powell's press conference is no more likely to reverse the statement reaction than to extend it. Don't assume "wait for the press conference to fade the initial move" as a rule; the data doesn't support it.

---

## The Day After: Which Face Does Next-Day NY AM Actually Follow?

If the statement and press conference don't agree with each other, the natural next question is whether the following morning eventually sides with one of them. Tested directly across all 43 FOMC decisions:

| | Next-day NY AM (9:30–11:00) direction matches... |
|---|---|
| **Stage 1 (statement)** | **55.8%** (CI 41.1–69.6%) |
| Stage 2 (press conference) | 51.2% (CI 36.8–65.4%) |

**A real but modest lean toward the statement, not the press conference.** In the specific cases where the two stages actively disagreed with each other (22 of 43 decisions — a genuine Vizard moment, two faces pulling opposite ways), next-day NY AM still sided with the statement slightly more often: **54.5%** (CI 34.7–73.1%). Both intervals are wide and overlap substantially — treat this as a lean worth watching, not a confirmed edge, but it's the first evidence in this framework that the statement carries marginally more lasting weight than the press conference commentary built on top of it.

### The RTH gap 50% level taps less reliably the morning after FOMC

| | RTH gap 50% (CE) tapped during next-day NY AM |
|---|---|
| Day after FOMC | **55.8%** (CI 41.1–69.6%) |
| Baseline (non-FOMC-adjacent days) | **65.5%** (CI 62.8–68.2%) |

A real, roughly 10-point gap. **This isn't because next-day NY AM is quieter — it's the opposite.**

| | Avg. NY AM range | Avg. \|net\| move | Directional efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Day after FOMC | **186pts (111.2% of baseline)** | **93pts (109.2%)** | 0.499 |
| Baseline | 167pts | 85pts | 0.508 |

Range and net move both run modestly larger than normal, with directional efficiency essentially unchanged (0.499 vs. 0.508 — no meaningful difference). **The honest read: the day after FOMC isn't choppier, it's carrying more directional momentum forward — the session moves further and stays away from the gap's midpoint rather than looping back through it, consistent with genuine follow-through from the prior day's event rather than a mean-reverting session.**

---

## Gap Fill Runs *Above* Baseline — The Opposite of Both Other Events

| | FOMC | Baseline | *(NFP)* | *(CPI)* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Full RTH gap fill | **73.0%** (CI 57.0–84.6%) | 57.7% | *37.8% — suppressed* | *53.8% — mildly suppressed* |

**This is the sharpest divergence from the other two models in this entire framework.** NFP and CPI both suppress gap-fill rates relative to baseline, because their catalysts hit right at or before the open and immediately establish a directional push that resists mean-reversion. FOMC's catalyst doesn't land until 2:00 PM — **a full four and a half hours of ordinary trading happens first**, giving the morning's opening gap ample time to close through completely normal price action before the actual news event ever occurs. The mechanism is different, so the outcome is different. Don't apply either Cero's or Gran Rey Cero's gap-suppression assumption to FOMC days.

---

## No Disruption Mechanic — Same Conclusion as CPI, Different From NFP

| | FOMC | *(NFP)* | *(CPI)* |
|---|---|---|---|
| Week's direction matches the release day's own direction | 46.5% (CI 32.5–61.1%) — near neutral | *42.9% — a real opposing lean* | *52.5% — neutral* |
| Next day continues the release day's own direction | **55.8%** (CI 41.1–69.6%) — leans toward continuation | *36.8% — leans strongly toward reversal* | *56.1% — leans toward continuation* |

**FOMC and CPI agree here, and both disagree with NFP.** Neither shows NFP's disruption signature — the idea that the event specifically attacks whoever is already positioned favorably. If anything, both lean mildly toward the next session continuing rather than reversing what the event day did. Cero's central organizing principle doesn't transfer to either of the other two release types.

---

## DXY — The Strongest Reaction and the Strongest Contrarian Relationship of All Three Events

| | FOMC | *(NFP)* | *(CPI)* |
|---|---|---|---|
| DXY RTH range vs. its own baseline | **192.5%** | *139.4%* | *116.6%* |
| Same-day NQ/DXY contrarian rate | **63.0%** (CI 44.2–78.5%) | *55.3%* | *57.1%* |
| DXY's own statement-vs-press-conference reversal | 48.1% — also a coin flip | — | — |

**This makes complete mechanical sense: FOMC is the actual rate-setting event, not secondhand data about the economy that feeds into a future decision.** DXY reacts to it more violently than to either NFP or CPI, nearly doubling its own baseline range. And because both DXY and equities are reacting to the same direct catalyst rather than each independently interpreting indirect data, the standard dollar-down/equities-up contrarian relationship sharpens here more than around either other event. DXY's own two-stage behavior mirrors NQ's — no reversal pattern between the statement and press-conference windows.

---

## What This Means in Practice

1. **Expect the sharpest pre-release quiet and the sharpest post-release expansion of any scheduled event in this framework.** NY AM ahead of FOMC runs meaningfully below normal; the two release windows run at 2.5–3x their own baseline.
2. **Use Asia's own range as an early read on the whole day.** A tight Asia session (at or below ~101pts) is a leading signal that London and NY AM will run at roughly half the range of a wide-Asia day — genuine information available hours before the release itself, and it scales through the press conference too. The one thing it doesn't predict is the statement window's own size, which holds close to full regardless.
2. **Don't assume a downward-biased day is unusual — but hold this one loosely.** The pooled sample leans down (41.9% up-close vs. 53.9% baseline), unlike NFP and CPI's upward lean — but this specific finding is carried almost entirely by 2018's eight meetings; 2022 and 2023–2026 each sit close to a coin flip on their own. The larger-than-normal displacement finding is more robust, but its size is driven mainly by 2022's extreme volatility, not a uniform effect.
3. **Don't trade the press conference expecting it to reverse the statement reaction.** Despite being a genuine second catalyst, the data shows no reversal tendency — treat the two windows as independent, not as a fade setup.
4. **If forced to lean on one face over the other going into the next session, lean toward the statement.** The edge is modest (55.8% vs. 51.2%, wide overlapping intervals) but it's the only evidence in this framework that one face carries more weight than the other.
5. **Don't expect the RTH gap to tap reliably during next-day NY AM.** It taps roughly 10 points less often than an ordinary day (55.8% vs. 65.5%) — not because the session is quieter, but because it's carrying real directional momentum forward from the FOMC event itself (range and net move both run ~110% of baseline, with unchanged directional efficiency).
4. **Don't expect the opening gap to stay open.** FOMC's gap-fill rate runs *above* normal, the opposite assumption from both Cero and Gran Rey Cero — the long pre-release trading window gives it time to close before the news even arrives.
5. **Don't import the NFP disruption mechanic.** Neither the week's direction nor the following session shows FOMC attacking existing positioning the way NFP does.
6. **DXY is the most reliable secondary read of the three events.** Its reaction is the largest and its contrarian relationship with NQ is the strongest — worth weighting more heavily here than around NFP or CPI.
7. **This model draws on a genuinely larger sample (43 events) than either Cero or Gran Rey Cero**, extending back to 2018 and 2022 specifically to achieve that — but the DXY comparisons above are still limited to 27 events (2023–2026 only), and one core finding (the directional down-lean) was tested for cross-period robustness and found to be substantially a 2018 artifact rather than a consistent pattern. Every other non-DXY finding held up when checked period by period.

---
*All confidence intervals are 95% Wilson score intervals. The 2018 and 2022 subsets were built from separate NQ datasets pulled specifically for this test and are not part of this framework's core 2023–2026 benchmark; they connect continuously into that benchmark from 2022 onward, while 2018 stands as an isolated block for week-structure and next-day tests.*
