# MODEL 6 — "CERO"
### NFP Day Price Delivery — A Disruption-Based System
**38 actual NFP Fridays in the dataset (2023–2026), out of 42 calendar-identified dates. Every figure here carries wider uncertainty than the other five models in this framework — stated once here, and implied throughout rather than repeated after every number.**

---

## Why This Name, and Why This Rewrite

A Cero is a single, concentrated blast — enormous, anticipated, over quickly. NFP is exactly that: a scheduled release that injects one sharp burst of disruption into an otherwise ordinary week.

This version of the model is built differently from the first draft. Rather than a list of independent findings, it's organized as a system — the way the concept is actually taught: a dealing range gets built before the release, liquidity above and below it gets graded by how close or far it sits, the release itself behaves like a disruption mechanism aimed at whoever is currently positioned well, and the day resolves in stages rather than a single clean move. Every piece of that framework was tested against the data rather than assumed. Some of it held up. Some of it needed correcting. Both are reported here.

---

## Stage 0 — What Kind of Day This Actually Is

NFP is not delivered the way an ordinary trading day is. Most days show a patient, orderly kind of price delivery — ranges build, levels get respected, reactions are proportionate. NFP behaves more like a manual override of that process: fast, capable of moving well outside normal boundaries, and genuinely hard to read with the precision this framework applies elsewhere.

Two things follow from that:

**First, this is a study day before it's a trading day.** The size and conviction applied here should be dramatically smaller than anywhere else in this framework — not because the mechanics aren't real, but because a 38-day sample of an unusually volatile event is inherently noisier than the 500–800+ day samples behind the other five models. Treat everything below as a genuine, real lean worth knowing — not a settled rule to trade at full size.

**Second, the actual jobs number is irrelevant to this system.** Nothing here depends on knowing what was reported, whether it beat or missed expectations, or why. Only the market's *reaction* — where it sweeps, where it stalls, where it reverses — carries information. This is a pure price-and-liquidity system, not a fundamental one.

## Stage 1 — Build the Dealing Range

Before 8:30, mark the premarket high and low from **7:00–9:00 AM ET**. This isn't just a directional bias tool — it's the actual container the release tests and, more often than not, breaks.

Session character confirms exactly this shape, and it's the single cleanest, most mechanically sensible result in this entire model:

| Session | NFP avg range vs. baseline |
|---|---|
| Asia | 93.8% — quieter than normal |
| London | 86.2% — meaningfully quieter |
| **NY AM (contains the release)** | **120.0% — the clear disruption window** |
| NY Lunch | 109.2% — still digesting |
| NY PM | 101.6% — back to normal |

The hours before the release are genuinely calmer than an ordinary day — consistent with positioning caution ahead of a known catalyst. The dealing range built in that quiet window is what gets tested once the release hits.

---

## Stage 2 — Grade the Liquidity: Minor vs. Primary

Not every level above and below the dealing range carries equal weight. The closer, more immediate reference — the premarket range's own high or low — is the **minor** pool. The more structurally significant, further-away reference — a prior day's high or low, a weekly relative-equal level — is the **primary** pool.

The instinct to expect is: minor gets tested first (fast, close, low resistance), primary gets tested only if the move genuinely has legs. Tested directly against the dealing range itself:

| | Result |
|---|---|
| Both sides of the range swept by end of RTH | 42.1% (CI 27.9–57.8%) |
| **Only one side swept** | **57.9% — the more common outcome** |
| Neither side swept | 0% |

Every NFP day sweeps *something* — that part isn't in question. But the idea of running the near side and then reversing to run the far side is a real pattern that happens on a real minority of days, not the default case. **Don't assume the primary/far target is coming just because the minor/near one already got hit.** Slightly better than 2-in-5.

---

## Stage 3 — The First 15 Minutes Are for Watching, Not Trading

The guidance to sit out the first 15 minutes after the release is common, and it's right — but tested directly, it's right for a different reason than usually given.

| Window | Matches the eventual RTH close direction |
|---|---|
| Raw initial spike (8:30–8:45) | 42.1% |
| **"Settled" window after waiting (8:45–9:00)** | **36.8% — worse, not better** |

Both sit below a coin flip, and waiting doesn't sharpen the read — it's marginally weaker. **The value of sitting out the first 15 minutes isn't a clearer signal — it's avoiding catastrophic fills and slippage while the market is at its most violent.** Worth being precise about that distinction: this is an execution-safety rule, not a signal-quality rule.

---

## Stage 4 — The Disruption Mechanic

This is the organizing idea behind the whole system, and it's the part the data confirms most convincingly, from three independent angles.

**The idea:** NFP doesn't primarily exist to *start* a new trend — it exists to disrupt whoever is already positioned comfortably. If the week has been grinding in one direction and traders are sitting on paper profits going into Friday, the release is disproportionately likely to move against that position, not extend it.

**Confirmation #1 — the week's own direction doesn't carry into NFP:**
The week's Monday–Thursday direction matched NFP's own direction only **42.9%** of the time (Part 4 detail below) — meaning NFP moved *against* the week's established direction on roughly 57% of Fridays.

**Confirmation #2 — NFP's own move doesn't carry into the following session either:**
The next trading day continued NFP's direction only **36.8%** of the time — meaning the session right after NFP disrupts NFP's own resolution nearly two-thirds of the time.

**Confirmation #3 — the disruption operates within the day itself, not just across days:**
Splitting the morning into two explicit stages — an initial push (8:30–8:50) and a second stage (8:50–11:00) — the second stage opposed the first **65.8%** of the time. The initial spike behaves like bait more often than confirmation.

**All three point the same direction, at three different timeframes: the week going in, the day itself, and the day coming out.** That consistency across genuinely independent tests is the strongest evidence in this whole model — treat "expect disruption, not continuation" as the central operating assumption for this system.

---

## Stage 5 — The Gap Doesn't Close the Way It Usually Does

Because the release lands at 8:30, a full hour before the 9:30 RTH open, a real gap frequently forms between the prior session's settlement and today's open. The instinct is to treat that gap the way gaps are normally treated — expect it to fill, at least partially, early in the session.

That instinct doesn't hold up here:

| | NFP | Baseline |
|---|---|---|
| Full gap fill (by end of RTH) | **37.8%** | 57.8% |
| 50%-fill specifically within the first 30 minutes (9:30–10:00) | **40.5%** | 52.6% |

Both figures run meaningfully below the already-modest non-NFP baseline — and well below the sometimes-cited expectation that a gap this size closes halfway within the opening half hour most of the time. **The release's disruption mechanic pushes price toward fresh ground rather than back through the gap.** Use the gap and its midpoint as reference levels, not as something to plan a trade around filling.

## Stage 6 — Everything Else Confirmed From the First Draft

The remaining findings from the original build hold and are carried forward unchanged, since nothing in the lecture-derived framework contradicted them:

**Prior weekday settlements get revisited far less than the general baseline** — Thursday's close 39.5%, Wednesday's 45.9%, Tuesday's 20.0%, Monday's 16.7%, all well under the general 61.1% ETH-settlement baseline. Consistent with Stage 4's disruption framing: price pushes toward new ground rather than back to old reference points.

**NWOG is already resolved by the time NFP happens** — filled essentially every time (100%, n=23) by Thursday, before the release even occurs. Not a live target on NFP day.

**True day open (midnight) tracks the general baseline** — 60.5% vs. the framework's general ~67.0%, within noise of normal behavior.

**The week doesn't compress predictably ahead of the release** — Wednesday and Thursday run slightly quieter (94–96% of baseline), but Tuesday is actually wider (111.5%), with no clean explanation. No hard "quiet before the storm" rule holds across the whole week.

**An already-volatile week produces a bigger NFP move, not a smaller one** — 399pts average range following a wide week vs. 289pts following a narrow one. This directly contradicts a coiled-spring story: volatility clusters rather than compresses and releases.

**Thursday's position within the week's range doesn't predict NFP's direction** — both the top-half and bottom-half mean-reversion tests landed squarely on noise (33.3% and 58.8%, both wide, overlapping intervals).

**The directional split itself is real and worth remembering on its own terms** — NFP closes up more often by frequency (63.2%) than the baseline (55.2%), but the average net move is actually slightly negative (−7.3 vs. +8.9 baseline), and absolute displacement runs about 21% larger than normal. Read together: more frequent small up-days, offset by less frequent but meaningfully larger down-days. Fatter downside tails, not a simple bullish lean.

## Stage 7 — Time and Price: Where in the Session the Day Actually Gets Built

Everything above establishes *that* NFP behaves differently from an ordinary day. This stage establishes *when*, within the session itself, that difference actually shows up — a genuine time-and-price decomposition of the RTH session rather than a single session-level average.

**NFP days front-load their range construction, completing more of the eventual day's full range earlier than an ordinary day, at every checkpoint tested:**

| Time | NFP: % of eventual full-day range already built | Baseline |
|---|---|---|
| 10:00 | 48.7% | 45.9% |
| 10:30 | 65.2% | 59.9% |
| 11:00 | 73.4% | 67.8% |
| 12:00 | 82.5% | 76.7% |
| 13:00 | 88.3% | 82.5% |
| 14:00 | 93.2% | 88.0% |
| 15:00 | 97.6% | 93.5% |

The gap is real and persists through the entire session rather than closing — by 11:00, NFP days have already built nearly three-quarters of their eventual range; an ordinary day is still five points behind that pace. **The catalyst front-loads the day's volatility; it doesn't spread it evenly.**

**The RTH low and the RTH high don't form with the same timing on NFP days.** The low is disproportionately likely to print early — **43.2% of NFP-day lows form in the first 30 minutes (9:30–10:00)**, against 32.3% at baseline. The high shows the opposite skew, disproportionately likely to print in the afternoon — **16.2% of NFP-day highs form between 14:00 and 15:00**, against 9.1% at baseline. **This is structurally consistent with Stage 6's own directional-split finding**: an early low followed by a higher-probability afternoon high is exactly the shape that would produce a more-often-up-close day built from smaller, more frequent gains rather than one big directional thrust.

**Two macro windows stand out clearly when tested for whether they reverse the trend already established earlier in the session — and they point in different directions from each other.**

| Window | NFP: reverses the pre-window trend | Baseline | NFP's share of the day's total range |
|---|---|---|---|
| 9:50–10:00 ("silver bullet") | **59.5%** | 47.6% | **38.0%** (vs. 34.1% baseline) |
| 10:50–11:10 | 51.4% | 46.1% | 26.8% (vs. 24.9%) |
| 11:30–13:30 (lunch) | **35.1%** | 48.7% | 46.6% (vs. 47.5%) |
| 13:50–14:10 | 47.2% | 48.3% | 18.0% (vs. 19.0%) |
| 14:50–15:10 | 44.4% | 48.1% | 15.3% (vs. 18.5%) |
| **15:15–15:45 (close-out)** | **75.0%** | 51.6% | 17.5% (vs. 21.5%) |

**The 9:50–10:10 silver-bullet window earns its reputation on NFP days specifically** — a real, elevated reversal rate (59.5% vs. 47.6%) that also carries a disproportionate share of the day's total range (38.0% vs. 34.1%), meaning this window isn't just more likely to reverse the trend, it's doing more of the day's actual work while it does it.

**The 15:15–15:45 close-out window shows the single largest gap of any window tested — a 23-point difference in reversal rate (75.0% vs. 51.6%)** — but, notably, with a *smaller* share of the day's range than baseline (17.5% vs. 21.5%). This is a distinct signature from the silver bullet: not a high-magnitude window, a high-*reliability* one. On NFP days, whatever direction the session has been leaning into the final 45 minutes is unusually likely to reverse, even though that reversal tends to be modest in size rather than a large late-day move.

**The lunch window (11:30–13:30) runs the opposite way from both of the above** — NFP days show a *lower* reversal rate here than baseline (35.1% vs. 48.7%), meaning whatever trend is in place by late morning is more likely to simply continue through lunch on NFP days than it is on an ordinary day. Don't apply the general "lunch reverts" assumption on NFP day specifically — the data says the opposite.

## Structural Confirmation Tools (Use, Don't Lead With)

Two tools from the broader framework are directly relevant here but weren't independently re-tested at NFP-specific scale in this document:

- **SMT divergence** (see Model 3, Genjutsu Kai) remains conceptually applicable during NFP — a fresh extreme in NQ that ES doesn't confirm is the same signal here as anywhere else in this framework. Use it as a secondary confirmation layer once a directional read is already forming from the stages above.
- **A balance price range** — several overlapping candles that have already built genuine positioning in a tight band — functions as a real structural wall. A failed attempt to break through it is legitimate rejection information. This is a visual, discretionary structure (a form of PD array) rather than something this framework tests directly from OHLC boundaries alone — apply your own confirmed identification method here, the same convention used throughout this project.

## The System, End to End

1. **Treat the day as a study exercise first.** Reduce size dramatically or work in simulation until your own extensive experience with this specific event justifies more.
2. **Build the dealing range** from the 7:00–9:00 AM premarket high and low, before the release.
3. **Grade the liquidity above and below it** — minor (near) vs. primary (far), including nearby prior-day and weekly levels.
4. **Do not act during 8:30–8:45.** This is for execution safety, not signal clarity — the "settled" read after waiting is no better, and slightly worse (36.8% vs. 42.1%).
5. **Expect disruption, not continuation — at every timeframe.** The week's own trend doesn't carry into NFP (42.9% continuation), NFP's own initial move doesn't carry into its second stage (34.2% continuation), and NFP's resolution doesn't carry into the next session (36.8% continuation). Three independent confirmations of the same mechanic.
6. **Don't expect the gap to close, even halfway, in the opening 30 minutes.** Both figures run below the general baseline, which is itself modest.
7. **NY AM is where the actual event lives** — roughly 20% wider than normal, bracketed by a quieter Asia/London before and a normalizing NY PM after.
8. **The day front-loads its range, not spreads it evenly.** By 11:00, NFP days have typically already built ~73% of their eventual full-day range against ordinary days' ~68% — expect the bulk of the day's true movement to already be in place by midday.
9. **Watch the RTH low early and the RTH high late.** The low disproportionately forms in the first 30 minutes (43.2% of days); the high disproportionately forms in the 14:00–15:00 window (16.2% of days) — consistent with the day's own up-close lean.
10. **The 9:50–10:10 silver bullet and the 15:15–15:45 close-out both carry real, elevated reversal tendency on NFP days (59.5% and 75.0% against baselines of 47.6% and 51.6%) — but don't apply the same logic to lunch.** The 11:30–13:30 window actually reverses *less* often than normal on NFP days (35.1% vs. 48.7%) — whatever's trending into lunch is more likely to keep going, not revert.
11. **Layer structural confirmation on top of the above** — SMT divergence and balance-range rejections — rather than trading off any single piece in isolation.
12. **Hold every number here loosely.** This is a 37–38-event sample describing a genuinely unusual day type — real, worth knowing, and still the least statistically settled model in this framework.
