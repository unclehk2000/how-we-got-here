# Midterm Election Years: 2018 vs 2022 — Comparative Time & Price Analysis

**Two US midterm election years, two very different market regimes.**

| | 2018 | 2022 |
|---|---|---|
| Election day | Nov 6 | Nov 8 |
| NQ full-year | 6,440 → 6,310 (**−2.0%**) | 16,383 → 11,340 (**−30.8%**) |
| Sessions analysed | 239 | 237 |
| Avg daily range | 114 pts | 320 pts |
| Avg weekly range | 277 pts | 769 pts |
| % weeks closing up | 51.0% | 40.0% |

**Critical framing:** 2018 was a *flat, choppy* year with a violent Q4; 2022 was a *sustained directional decline* all year. Both were midterm years, but "midterm year" clearly did not produce a common market character. Any pattern that shows up in both is far more likely to be structural than election-driven.

## The relationship between volatility and direction

**Volatility clusters with decline, in both years — but not perfectly.**

- **2018:** the two widest months (Oct −9.6%, Dec −11.1%) were the two worst-performing months. The quietest months (Sept, June) were near-flat.
- **2022:** the widest month (Jan −9.3%) and second-widest (Apr −13.4%) were both large declines. But **March (+5.25%) ranked 3rd widest and July (+12.95%) ranked 8th** — 2022's biggest *up* month was a mid-tier volatility month.

**The honest read:** wide-range months skew heavily toward declines, but width alone doesn't tell you direction. 2022's July is the counterexample — a nearly 13% rally on merely average volatility. Range tells you how much is available, not which way.

**Seasonal note that does NOT transfer:** October was 2018's most volatile month and 2022's *second-quietest*. December was 2018's #2 and 2022's #12 (last). There is no consistent monthly volatility calendar across these two midterm years.

# PART 2 — THE ELECTION WINDOW

## 2018 (election Nov 6)

| Window | Avg range | Avg net | % up days | vs. year baseline |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 days pre | 214 | −13 | 60% | **187%** |
| 10 days post | 148 | −9 | 40% | **129%** |

## 2022 (election Nov 8)

| Window | Avg range | Avg net | % up days | vs. year baseline |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 days pre | 305 | −70 | 30% | **95%** |
| 10 days post | 239 | +27 | 50% | **74%** |

## What actually happened around the elections

**The single most consistent finding: volatility contracted after the election in both years.** 2018 dropped from 187% of baseline to 129%; 2022 dropped from 95% to 74%. Post-election ranges were roughly **30% and 22% narrower** than pre-election ranges respectively.

**Election day itself was quiet in both years:**
- 2018 election day range: **102 pts** — the *smallest* range in the entire 21-day window
- 2022 election day range: 302 pts, and it closed **exactly flat** (net −0.4 pts on the day)

**But the directional aftermath was completely different:**
- **2018:** post-election was weak — 40% up days, and the market rolled over into what became a −11% December
- **2022:** post-election was constructive — 50% up days, +27 avg net, and the two days after (Nov 10 +2.39%, Nov 11 +1.93%) were sharp rallies

**Interpretation, offered with appropriate caution:** the volatility-contraction-after-election pattern showed up in both years and has a sensible mechanism — event risk resolving. That's the one piece of this section worth carrying forward. The *directional* aftermath was opposite in the two years, which means midterm elections do not appear to carry a reliable directional signal. Two observations is also an extremely small sample; treat all of this as descriptive of what happened rather than predictive of what will.

# PART 3 — WEEKLY MARKET STRUCTURE

| Metric | 2018 | 2022 | Bull years (2023–26) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Avg weekly range | 277 | 769 | — |
| % weeks up | 51.0% | 40.0% | — |
| Prior week HIGH revisited | 54.0% | 49.0% | **63.7%** |
| Prior week LOW revisited | 42.0% | 53.1% | **38.0%** |
| Weekly direction persists | 54.0% | 51.0% | — |

**The prior-week-level asymmetry tracks the trend, confirming the earlier finding.** In the 2023–26 uptrend, the prior week's *high* was revisited far more (63.7% vs 38.0%). In 2022's decline that inverted (49.0% vs 53.1%). In 2018's flat year it sits between the two, mildly favouring the high (54.0% vs 42.0%) in what was a roughly sideways market.

**Weekly direction persistence is a coin flip in both midterm years** — 54.0% and 51.0%, both with confidence intervals comfortably containing 50%. Week-to-week momentum is not a usable signal in either regime.

# PART 4 — SESSION-LEVEL COMPARISON

| Session | 2018 range | 2022 range | 2018 %up | 2022 %up |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Asia | 31 | 81 | 53.8% | 48.9% |
| London | 32 | 107 | 52.5% | 50.2% |
| NY AM | 66 | 195 | 56.7% | 48.1% |
| NY Lunch | 45 | 117 | 49.6% | 46.0% |
| NY PM | 61 | 169 | 51.7% | 51.9% |

2022 ran roughly **2.6–3.3x** the session volatility of 2018 across the board.

## The setups, tested in both midterm years

| Setup | 2018 | 2022 | Bull years |
|---|---|---|---|
| **London opening-block breakout** | **82.5%** | **81.7%** | **79.5%** |
| NY PM opening-block breakout | 79.6% | 76.5% | 69.6% |
| NY AM opening-block breakout | 59.9% | 60.8% | 60.8% |
| Asia sweep → NY AM reverses | 31.8% | 27.8% | 31.6% |
| London sweep → NY AM reverses | 26.4% | 27.8% | 26.0% |

### This is the most important table in the document

**London opening-block breakout: 82.5% / 81.7% / 79.5%.** Six calendar years now — 2018, 2022, 2023, 2024, 2025, 2026 — spanning a flat year, a −30.8% collapse, and a multi-year rally. It has never left a 79–83% band. This is no longer a promising statistic; it is the most robust finding in this entire body of work.

**NY AM opening-block breakout: 59.9% / 60.8% / 60.8%.** Almost eerily stable, and stably mediocre. The most volatile session remains the least directionally reliable in every regime tested.

**Sweep-then-reverse: 26–32% in every single year tested.** The fade-the-sweep pattern has now failed across six years and three distinct regimes at a remarkably consistent rate. Continuation beats reversal roughly 2.5–3x regardless of market conditions.

## The carryover finding, now clarified

| Test | 2018 (flat) | 2022 (strong downtrend) | Bull years |
|---|---|---|---|
| NY AM → NY PM | **52.3%** | **61.6%** | 49.3% |
| London → NY AM | 49.2% | 55.7% | 48.2% |

This resolves the open question from the 2022 analysis. Session carryover appeared in 2022 (61.6%) but was flagged as possibly a one-year artifact.

**2018 settles it: carryover is a trend-strength effect, not a bear-market effect.** In 2018 — a down year, but a flat and choppy one — carryover was 52.3%, a coin flip, matching the bull years. It only appeared in 2022's sustained one-way decline.

**Conclusion:** session-to-session carryover works only in *strongly trending* markets, regardless of direction. It is a regime indicator, not a setup. If you notice it starting to work, that is evidence you are in a persistent trend — which is useful information in itself.

# PART 5 — WHAT THIS ADDS

### Confirmed as structural (now across 6 years, 3 regimes)
1. **London opening-block breakout** — 79.5–82.5% in every year tested
2. **Sweep continuation over reversal** — 26–32% reversal rate in every year tested
3. **NY AM breakout mediocrity** — ~60% in every year tested, despite highest volatility

### Confirmed as trend-dependent
4. **Prior-week level asymmetry** — follows the prevailing trend direction
5. **Session carryover** — appears only in strongly trending markets (2022 yes, 2018 no)

### Election-specific, weak evidence
6. **Volatility contracts post-election** — happened in both years (−30%, −22% vs pre-election), sensible mechanism, but n=2
7. **Directional aftermath** — opposite in the two years. No usable signal.

### Explicitly does NOT transfer
8. **Monthly volatility calendar** — October was 2018's most volatile month and 2022's second-quietest. December ranked #2 and #12. Do not build a monthly volatility expectation from midterm-year precedent.

---

## A note on "midterm year" as a category

The two midterm years in this dataset had almost nothing in common at the market level: −2.0% vs −30.8%, 114 vs 320 average daily range, opposite post-election direction, and completely inverted monthly volatility rankings.

The setups that worked, worked in both — but they also worked in the four non-midterm years. **Nothing here suggests midterm status itself is a tradeable variable.** The value of this comparison lies in what it proves about regime-robustness, not in what it says about elections.
